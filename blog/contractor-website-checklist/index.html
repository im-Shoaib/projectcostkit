Contractor Website Checklist: 15 Things That Bring In Calls

What a painter, plumber or handyman website actually needs to turn visitors into calls: pages, trust signals, speed, Google setup and mistakes to avoid.

By ProjectCostKitOct 20, 20265 min read

The short version

  • A contractor site has one job: get the visitor to call or request a quote. Put the phone number and a quote button on every screen.
  • One page per service and real photos of your work do more than any design trick.
  • Fast loading on a phone matters. Google says Core Web Vitals are used by its ranking systems.

Most homeowners look up a contractor on their phone, often right after getting your name from a neighbor or seeing your truck. In that moment your website has one job: convince them to call you instead of the next name. This checklist covers what a home service business site needs to do that, in rough order of importance.

The essentials

1. Your phone number, tappable, on every page

Put it at the top, in a sticky header on phones, so one tap starts a call. Add a "Request a quote" button next to it. Many visitors will never scroll.

2. What you do and where, in the first screen

"Interior and exterior painting in Columbus and nearby suburbs" says more than "Quality you can trust." A visitor should know in two seconds whether you do their job in their area.

3. One page for each main service

A separate page for each service you want to be found for, such as "cabinet painting" or "deck staining," with real details: what is included, your process, typical timelines and photos of that kind of work. It gives Google a page that matches what people search for, and gives the visitor a page that matches what they need.

4. Real photos of your own work

Before-and-after photos of real jobs beat stock photos every time. Name the town or neighborhood in the caption if the client agrees.

5. Reviews and proof

Show reviews, ideally linked to your Google Business Profile. Note that Google does not show review stars in search results for reviews a business puts on its own site about itself, so focus on reviews for the people reading your page, not for rich results.

6. License, insurance and guarantees

List your license number if your trade and state require one, say you are insured, and explain any warranty in plain words. These answer the questions people are afraid to ask.

7. A short quote form

Name, phone, address or zip code, the service and a short description. Let people attach photos if you can. Every extra required field loses some visitors.

8. A service area page or section

List the towns you cover. If you are a service-area business on Google Business Profile, keep the website's list consistent with the areas in your profile.

Speed and mobile

9. Fast on a phone

Google says its ranking systems use Core Web Vitals, a set of real-world measures of loading speed, responsiveness and visual stability, though good scores alone do not guarantee rankings. In practice, heavy page builders, auto-playing video and huge images make contractor sites slow. Compress your photos and keep the page lean.

10. Easy to read and tap

Big enough text, buttons a thumb can hit, no pop-up covering the page the moment it loads. Google's page experience guidance asks site owners to avoid intrusive interstitials that make content less accessible.

11. HTTPS

The padlock in the address bar. Most hosts include it free now, and browsers warn visitors about sites without it.

Getting found

12. A Google Business Profile that matches the site

Same business name, phone and service areas on both. The Google Business Profile guide for contractors covers setup and the rules that get profiles suspended.

13. Clear page titles

Each page title should say the service and the area, such as "Cabinet Painting in Columbus, OH | Your Business." That title is what shows up as the blue link in Google.

14. Helpful content

A few pages that answer real customer questions, like what a job costs or how long it takes, build trust and can bring in search traffic. Cost calculators work well for this; it is why sites like this one exist.

Keep it working

15. Check your forms and calls

Submit your own quote form every month. A broken form can silently cost you weeks of leads. Make sure messages reach an inbox you actually read.

Common mistakes

  • A phone number buried at the bottom of the page, or not clickable.
  • No prices or ranges anywhere, so people who want a ballpark leave.
  • A website built years ago that does not work on phones.
  • Stock photos of smiling models in hard hats.
  • A Facebook page used as the website, so the business is hard to find in search.
  • Contact forms that go to an email nobody checks.

Frequently asked questions

Do contractors really need a website if they have Facebook?

A social page helps, but a website you own gives you service pages for search, a place for quote requests and full control. Google's Business Profile rules also ask for a website that represents your location, not a redirect to a social page.

How many pages does a contractor website need?

At minimum: home, one page per main service, a service area section, reviews or past work, and contact. A small trade business can do well with a handful of focused pages; add more as you add services.

What should a contractor website cost?

It depends on the number of pages, photos and features, and who builds it. A simple, fast site with a few service pages costs far less than a large custom build. Get a fixed quote.

How long until a new website shows up on Google?

New sites can take weeks to months to rank for competitive searches. A complete Google Business Profile often brings calls sooner in local results.
